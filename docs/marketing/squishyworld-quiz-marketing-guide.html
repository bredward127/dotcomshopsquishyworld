Why a quiz converts

  • It replaces a choice with an answer. Amazon search gives thousands of near-identical squishies. The quiz returns three, with reasons.
  • Commitment builds. Four taps and a short wait make the result feel earned. Protect that: never add an email gate in front of results.
  • Three tiers catch three budgets. Best Match is the pick, Quiet Alternative handles the "but it's for school" objection, and Multi-Pack Value covers classrooms and gifting.

In Associates Central, open the tracking-ID report and break it down by sub-tag. That gives earnings per profile, per results tier, and per campaign, which analytics alone cannot.

UTM rules

  • Use lowercase letters and numbers only: utm_source=meta, utm_campaign=springlaunch. Punctuation is stripped from the sub-tag, and each part is cut to 24 characters.
  • Sources: meta, tiktok, pinterest, google, newsletter, organic.
  • Put the creative ID in utm_content (for example c014). It appears in analytics, not in the sub-tag.

03 · What the benchmarks say

Creative testing is a numbers game

The takeaways below come from Creative Benchmarks 2026. A "winner" there means an ad that spent at least 10× its account's median ad and at least $500. All findings are associations across accounts, measured by spend, and they come from a window that includes BFCM.

~5%
of ads become winners. Low hit rates are normal, not a sign of weak creative.
~55%
of all spend goes to winning ads. About 28% goes to mid-range and 17% to losers.
1 in 10–13
is the most you should expect to be winners on average.

Volume is associated with more winners

Accounts that launch more creatives per week tend to find more winners, including among advertisers with similar budgets. That doesn't prove more ads cause more winners, but it is the most consistent pattern in the data. For a new affiliate funnel, the relevant tiers are Micro and Small:

Source: report charts CH-003 and CH-008. Top 25% = accounts in the top quartile by winner count within their tier.

Mid-range ads are not failures

In Micro accounts, about 46% of creatives are mid-range (28+ days of spend, not winners), and they take about 46% of spend. Winners take only about 23% there. At small budgets, steady mid-range ads carry the account, so don't switch off an ad that keeps spending just because it isn't a breakout.

Source: CH-005 and CH-006, Micro tier: loser 50.2% / mid 46.0% / winner 3.7% of creatives; 31.5% / 45.6% / 23.0% of spend.

Hit rate is not a report card

A high hit rate can simply mean too little testing. Judge the program on winners per month and earnings per sub-tag, not on the share of ads that worked.

Formats and hooks that show up among winners

  • Offer-first banners and demos rank near the top on both hit rate and spend-use ratio (scale formats).
  • Text-forward assets (text-only, product image with text, simple GIFs) appear among winners more often than many teams expect. Their strength is speed and clarity.
  • Unboxing, POV, and behind-the-scenes formats have high hit rates but lower spend use.
  • Hooks with immediacy and clarity (a concrete reason to act, price framing, newness) surface often. Curiosity and confessional hooks can stop the scroll.

Source: CH-009, CH-011, CH-012. Patterns are time-bound (holiday window) and vary by vertical. Test them; don't assume them.

04 · Creative playbook

Angles, hooks, and formats

Build each week's batch as profile × format. Four profiles and a few cheap formats give you enough variety to reach the 5-per-week pace without a studio.

Quiet Desk Focus

Angle: busy hands, quiet room.

  • "Your hands need something to do in meetings. Here's the silent one."
  • "4 questions to find a fidget nobody on the call will hear."
  • Demo: a squeeze next to a laptop mic with the waveform staying flat.
Slow-Rise Calm Keeper

Angle: soft, slow, always within reach.

  • Close-up slow-rise timelapse with the text "Watch it come back."
  • "The backpack squishy that survives the school run."
  • POV: bedtime wind-down, one squishy on the pillow.
Deep Compression Seeker

Angle: the harder the squeeze, the better.

  • "Most stress balls give up. This one pushes back."
  • Demo: full-grip squeeze, slow-motion rebound.
  • Confessional: "I've destroyed 6 stress balls. The quiz found the 7th."
Texture Explorer

Angle: every surface is worth a poke.

  • ASMR-style texture montage: gel, beads, nubs, stretch.
  • Unboxing a multi-pack: "Which one did the quiz pick?"
  • "Rinse it, squish it again. Washable textures only."

Quiz-first creative (use for every profile)

  • Question as hook: "Slow-rise, heavy squeeze, or gel? Take the 60-second quiz."
  • Result reveal: screen-record the quiz on a phone, ending on the persona card.
  • Text-only static: cream background, one line in Fredoka: "Find your squishy in 4 taps."
  • Offer-first framing without a fake discount: "3 hand-matched picks. No email. Free." (the quiz is the offer).

05 · Channels

Where the traffic comes from

Meta (Facebook + Instagram): primary paid channel

  • Optimize for landing-page views or a custom conversion on quiz_complete once it has volume. Don't optimize for Amazon purchases, which you can't see.
  • Use broad targeting with creative doing the segmenting (see section 07 on health-based targeting).
  • One campaign, one ad set per budget test, new creatives added weekly. Retire only ads that stop spending, not steady mid-range ads.

TikTok and Instagram Reels: organic first

  • Texture and slow-rise content is native to these feeds. Post the demo and ASMR cuts organically, then put paid spend behind the ones that pull.
  • Link-in-bio goes to /quiz?utm_source=tiktok&utm_campaign=bio.

Pinterest: evergreen affiliate traffic

  • Pin boards per profile: "Quiet fidgets for the office", "Calm-down corner ideas", "Sensory toys for texture lovers".
  • Pins link to the quiz, not to Amazon, so every click passes through your tag and sub-tag.

Your own site: the Sensory Library

  • The home page already links to the quiz. Add a short, clearly labeled "Looking for a fidget?" callout to relevant library articles (proprioception, heavy work, classroom calm).
  • Keep the informational pages free of affiliate links, as the disclosure page promises. Link to the quiz instead.

Google Search: test carefully

  • Target generic intent such as "quiet fidget for office" or "slow rise squishy for kids".
  • Never bid on Amazon trademark terms (see section 07).

06 · Operating rhythm

The weekly loop

Monday: read
Fill in the scorecard. Pull Associates earnings by sub-tag. Note which profile won the week.
Tuesday: make
Produce ~5 new creatives using the batch template, weighted toward the winning profile but never only that one.
Wednesday: launch
Add the new ads with fresh utm_content IDs. Leave steady spenders running.
Friday: tune the page
Check drop-off by quiz_step. Change at most one thing on /quiz per week so you can tell what worked.

Page experiments worth running (one at a time)

  1. Hero headline: "60 seconds" vs. "4 taps" vs. a profile-specific headline for each ad set.
  2. Product picks: swap in real ASINs whose ratings you have checked yourself, then compare click rates by slot.
  3. Results order on mobile: Best Match first (current) vs. Quiet Alternative first for classroom-heavy traffic.
  4. Question 1 copy: test clearer "who is it for" options if step-1 drop-off is high.

Monthly review

  • Winners per month, compared with the tier table in section 03 (Micro top 25%: about 4.8 creatives per week).
  • Earnings by profile. Is one profile under-served by its product picks?
  • Refresh products: check that every ASIN is in stock and still well reviewed.

07 · Compliance

Rules that keep the account alive

Health claims: the biggest risk

  • Squishies are comfort and fidget tools. Never say or imply they treat, cure, or manage anxiety, ADHD, autism, or sensory processing disorder.
  • Say "for focus", "for fidgeting", or "a calming squeeze". Don't say "reduces anxiety" or "therapy tool".
  • Don't use badges like "Tested for Sensory Regulation" or "Non-Toxic" unless you hold documentation for that specific product. The quiz ships with verifiable trust badges instead.

Ad platform targeting and copy

  • Meta: ad copy must not assert or imply personal attributes, including health. "Do you have anxiety?" is not allowed; "Restless hands in meetings?" is.
  • Google: personalized-ads policy restricts health-based targeting and remarketing. Target intent (keywords, topics), not conditions.
  • Avoid audiences built from people who visited health-condition content on your site.

Amazon Associates

  • Keep the disclosure "As an Amazon Associate we earn from qualifying purchases" visible. The quiz shows it in the top strip and footer, and labels every CTA.
  • Send paid traffic to your own page (/quiz), not directly to Amazon links.
  • Don't bid on "Amazon" or Amazon trademark keywords, and don't put affiliate links in emails, PDFs, or offline material.
  • Prices: Amazon's rules limit displaying Amazon prices that don't come from its Product Advertising API. The quiz shows typical category ranges and sends shoppers to the live price. If you'd rather avoid any ambiguity, remove the ranges or switch to the API.
  • Re-read the current Associates Program Operating Agreement before launch. It changes, and it is the final word.

Honesty in creative

  • No invented reviews, ratings, "10,000+ happy customers" counts, or fake discounts. The FTC treats fabricated reviews and endorsements as deceptive.
  • UGC and creator content must disclose paid relationships (#ad or the platform's paid-partnership label).
  • The page promises that commission never picks the match. Keep it true: scoring is a fixed formula in lib/quiz/scoring.ts.

This guide is operational advice, not legal advice. For anything that touches health claims or children's products, get a review from someone qualified.
